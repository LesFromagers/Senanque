**1950 — The Streak That Almost Was Forever**
Bud Wilkinson's Sooners rolled through a perfect regular season, stacking their winning streak past thirty games and claiming the national title before a single bowl snap. Then Kentucky happened. A New Year's Day letdown in the Sugar Bowl snapped the run at the worst possible moment, a reminder that dynasties get humbled too. The title still counted — AP and UP both had OU number one — but Sooner fans of a certain age still wince at the mention of Kentucky.

**1955 — Ten Consecutive Big Sevens and Counting**
This was the season the streak stopped being a story and started being an institution — eleven wins, zero losses, and outscoring opponents by a combined 385 to 60. Tommy McDonald and Bo Bolinger anchored a team that had simply stopped losing. Down 6-0 at halftime of the Orange Bowl against Maryland, Wilkinson's men answered with twenty unanswered points, because apparently trailing at the half was just a formality that year.

**1956 — Forty and Counting, No Bowl Required**
Somehow OU got even better, running the win streak to forty and finishing consensus national champions without playing a single postseason game — the Big Seven's no-repeat rule sent Colorado to the Orange Bowl instead. Jerry Tubbs, a Heisman finalist at center of all places, headlined an All-America class that made the Sooners nearly impossible to stop. It's the strangest kind of dominance: too good for your own conference's rules to let you finish the job on the field.

**1969 — One Heisman, One Wild Season**
Steve Owens put together a season for the ages even as the team around him lurched between blowout wins and lopsided losses. He punished Oklahoma State for 261 rushing yards, set a Big Eight scoring record, and carried home the Heisman on the strength of sheer workload. The team finished 6-4 and shared the conference title anyway — proof that one player's greatness and a program's overall record don't always tell the same story.

**1971 — The Game of the Century**
Thanksgiving Day, fifty-five million people watching, and Oklahoma trading haymakers with number-one Nebraska in what's still argued as the greatest regular-season college football game ever played. Greg Pruitt and Jack Mildren gave OU the lead late, but Nebraska's Jeff Kinney found the end zone with barely more than a minute left to steal it, 35-31. Oklahoma answered by torching everyone else on the schedule, including a Sugar Bowl rout of Auburn, but "Sooner Magic" needed one more year to finish what this game started.

**1974 — Champions On Probation**
The NCAA had OU banned from bowls and television, so Barry Switzer's second team as head coach just went out and won anyway — eleven straight, an average of 43 points a game, and a wishbone attack that ran the ball nearly 74 times a contest. When Alabama stumbled in the Orange Bowl, Oklahoma stood alone as the nation's only unbeaten team and the AP crowned them champions with no bowl trophy in sight. It remains one of the strangest national titles in the sport's history: earned entirely off television, in front of nobody watching at home.

**1975 — Redemption at Kansas's Expense**
A stunning 23-3 loss at Kansas midseason looked like it might sink the whole campaign, but Oklahoma turned around and dismantled everyone left on the schedule, Nebraska included, to claim the Big Eight crown. Lee Roy Selmon's Outland and Lombardi-winning season anchored a defense that shut down Michigan in the Orange Bowl for Switzer's first bowl win. A late-breaking assist from UCLA, which upset top-ranked Ohio State, sealed OU's fifth national championship.

**1978 — Billy Sims and the Nebraska Rematch**
Billy Sims led the country in scoring and rushing on his way to the Heisman, powering Oklahoma through nine straight wins before Nebraska handed the Sooners their only regular-season loss. OU didn't have to wait long for a chance to answer: the same two teams met again in the Orange Bowl, and this time Oklahoma left no doubt, winning the rematch outright. Few storylines capture "Sooner Magic" better than turning a heartbreak loss into a bowl-game statement a few weeks later.

**1985 — Sixth Star on the Wall**
One loss all season, and it came at home to Miami — a defeat that stung but never derailed the larger picture. Switzer's Sooners steamrolled the rest of the schedule and closed it out by beating Penn State in the Orange Bowl to claim OU's sixth consensus national championship. This is the "Coronation at the Palace on the Prairie" era in its purest form: dominant, occasionally vulnerable, and always finding its way back to the top of the mountain.

**1987 — Game of the Century II, Then Heartbreak**
Oklahoma opened the season 11-0 and got a measure of revenge over top-ranked Nebraska in a rematch fans still call "Game of the Century II." But a third straight national-title dream died in the Orange Bowl against Miami, a Hurricanes program that had OU's number for three years running. Rickey Dixon's Jim Thorpe Award-winning season was a bright spot in a year that ultimately ended one win short of the crystal ball. *(Note: the exact Orange Bowl final score needs the manual check flagged in the gap report before this paragraph is finalized.)*

**1988 — The End of an Era**
Switzer's final season on the sideline delivered a nine-win campaign overshadowed almost entirely by what happened off the field — a booster scandal, a player's drug arrest, and a program suddenly staring down NCAA probation. It's not a season Sooner fans reach for first, but it belongs in the ledger precisely because it marks the hinge point between the Switzer dynasty and whatever came next. Great programs have hard chapters too, and this was one of OU's hardest.

**2000 — Perfect, and Nobody Saw It Coming**
Bob Stoops, in only his second year, took a team picked to finish mid-pack in the Big 12 and rode it to a 13-0 season and a national championship — beating a murderer's row of ranked opponents along the way, including number-one Nebraska and a rematch with number-two Florida State in the BCS title game. Josh Heupel quarterbacked the whole improbable run. It was OU's first title since 1985, and it announced that the Stoops era would be no rebuilding project.

**2003 — Jason White's Heisman, and a Near-Miss Title**
Jason White won the Heisman behind a offense that scored 601 points across the season and blew out nearly everyone in front of it, including a 65-13 demolition of Texas. But the storybook ending slipped away in back-to-back losses — to Kansas State in the Big 12 title game, then LSU in the BCS Championship. A dominant regular season doesn't always end the way it should, and 2003 is the reminder that the ledger has to hold the near-misses alongside the trophies. *(Note: this paragraph assumes the corrected 12-2 record flagged in the gap report.)*

**2008 — 716 Points and a Heisman Sophomore**
Sam Bradford's sophomore Heisman season fueled an offense so explosive it set an NCAA scoring record, averaging over 55 points a game across the full schedule. Oklahoma steamrolled its way to a Big 12 title, then met Tim Tebow's Florida Gators in the BCS Championship — a rare matchup of back-to-back Heisman winners — and came up just short. The scoreboard numbers alone make this one of the most offensively dominant seasons in program history, title or no title. *(Note: this paragraph assumes the corrected 12-2 record flagged in the gap report.)*

**2017 — Baker Mayfield Arrives**
Lincoln Riley's very first season as head coach delivered a Big 12 championship and a spot in the College Football Playoff, powered by Baker Mayfield's record-setting Heisman campaign. An upset loss at Iowa State was the only real speed bump before a classic Rose Bowl semifinal against Georgia, decided in double overtime on a Sony Michel touchdown run. It's remembered as much for the way it ended as for how good the season actually was — a genuine instant classic.

**2018 — Back-to-Back Heismans**
Kyler Murray took the baton from Baker Mayfield and won the Heisman himself, making Oklahoma the first program ever to produce back-to-back Heisman-winning quarterbacks. The Sooners lost to Texas in the regular season and then beat them right back in the Big 12 Championship, a split series that says a lot about how volatile that rivalry can be in a single year. A CFP semifinal loss to Alabama in the Orange Bowl closed the book, but the two-Heismans-in-a-row story is the one this season will always be remembered for.

**1953 — Where the Streak Started**
Bud Wilkinson's team opened the season by losing to top-ranked Notre Dame and then playing Pittsburgh to a tie the following week — an inauspicious start for what would become the most famous streak in college football history. A win over Texas the next Saturday turned out to be the first domino, and the Sooners reeled off victory after victory the rest of the way, capping it with a shutout of unbeaten Maryland in the Orange Bowl. Nobody in the stadium that day could have known they'd just watched the opening chapter of 47 straight wins. *(Note: several mid-season games in this draft still need confirming per the gap report — treat the streak framing as solid, the game-by-game details as provisional.)*

**1958 — The Streak Finally Breaks**
After the better part of five seasons without a loss, Oklahoma's remarkable run came to an end against Notre Dame — the same program that had handed Wilkinson his last defeat back in 1953, in a strange bit of symmetry. The Sooners still finished the year as Big Seven and Orange Bowl champions, a reminder that even the greatest streaks are chapters, not the whole story. *(Note: this draft is thin on purpose — the gap report flags that the full schedule and final record didn't come through this pull; fill in the actual streak-ending score before using this paragraph.)*

**1963 — Wilkinson's Last Ride**
In his seventeenth and final season roaming the Oklahoma sideline, Bud Wilkinson's Sooners knocked off a top-ranked USC team on the road before a heartbreaking loss to Texas in the Red River Shootout took the wind out of a promising campaign. An eight-win season was a quiet way for a legend to close out an era that had included three national titles and the 47-game streak. Wilkinson's retirement marked the true end of Oklahoma's first golden age.

**1980 — The Comeback Big Eight Title**
Two early losses, to Stanford and then to Texas, looked like they might sink the season before it started, but Barry Switzer's Sooners answered by winning eight games in a row and running the conference table. A dominant Orange Bowl win over Florida State closed out the year with OU ranked third nationally, proof that a rocky start doesn't have to define a season. It's the kind of resilience "Sooner Magic" gets named after.

**1986 — The Boz and the Best Defense in the Country**
Led by the swagger of Brian Bosworth and a defense that posted five shutouts on its way to leading the nation in every major defensive category, Oklahoma bounced back from an early loss at Miami to win its final ten games. The Sooners closed the year by blowing out Arkansas in the Orange Bowl, finishing third nationally on the back of one of the most dominant defensive units the program has ever fielded. It's the season "Sooner Magic" wore a mohawk and talked more trash than anyone in the country.

**2015 — Baker's Coming-Out Party**
A year before he'd win the Heisman, Baker Mayfield quarterbacked Oklahoma to its ninth Big 12 title and a spot in the College Football Playoff, capping an 11-win regular season that included a signature road win at Kansas State and a two-overtime thriller at Tennessee. The magic ran out in Miami, though, where Clemson's defense throttled the Sooners in the Orange Bowl semifinal. Still, this was the season that made it clear Mayfield — and Lincoln Riley's eventual offense — was going to be must-watch football for years to come.

**2016 — Stoops' Tenth Title**
Bob Stoops' next-to-last season opened rough — non-conference losses to Houston and Ohio State had the Sooners unranked before October — but Oklahoma answered by ripping off nine straight conference wins, capping it with a Sugar Bowl blowout of Auburn. It was the tenth conference championship of Stoops' eighteen years in Norman, a number that puts his run among the best in program history even without a national title to show for this particular season. The Playoff committee left OU out anyway, a reminder that in the four-team era, a great season doesn't always get you a ticket to the dance.
